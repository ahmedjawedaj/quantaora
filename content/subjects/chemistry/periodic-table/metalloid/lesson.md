# Metalloid

*Chemistry > Periodic Table > Reading the Periodic Table > The great elemental families > Metalloid*

A metalloid is an element with properties between metals and nonmetals. The metalloids sit along the table's staircase, and their part-way conductivity makes them semiconductors — the family that powers your phone.

## 1. See it

Look at the 3D model: a gleaming-but-not-bright silicon wafer floating above a periodic table, with the staircase of metalloid tiles glowing. Tap the `hs-metalloid-silicon` hotspot to open its card. Notice its soft metallic shine.

## 2. Touch it

Rotate the wafer and the table. Toggle the 'staircase' layer to trace the diagonal zigzag of metalloid tiles on the table. Toggle the 'conductor meter' layer to show a meter — metals at full, metalloids half, nonmetals near zero — so you can see the metalloid's in-between position.

## 3. Name it

Tap `hs-metalloid-silicon` to label the silicon wafer, `hs-metalloid-staircase` to label the staircase of metalloids on the table, and `hs-metalloid-semi` to label the half-conducting meter.

## 4. Explain what it does

Walk through what makes a metalloid: its properties sit between metals and nonmetals, it lives on the table's staircase, and its most famous trait is semiconduction — conducting only part-way and being switchable, which is exactly what computer chips need.

**Prediction prompt:** Before you toggle the conductor-meter layer on, predict where on the meter a metalloid's conductivity will fall compared with a metal — then look.

## 5. See where it belongs

The metalloids belong to the **periodic table** as the family along its diagonal staircase. From there they connect to the **metals** on their left and the **nonmetals** on their right, and upward to the **semiconductor** devices they power.

## 6. Compare it

Compare a **metalloid** with a **metal**. A metal conducts electricity fully — its meter pins to the top. A metalloid conducts only part-way — its meter rests at half. Both may look shiny, but only the metal is a full conductor; the metalloid's in-between state is what makes it a semiconductor.

## 7. Use it in a task

Use the model to answer a real question: *Why is silicon, a metalloid, used in computer chips instead of copper, a metal, or oxygen, a nonmetal?* Point to the conductor meter and explain why the metalloid's switchable, in-between conductivity wins.

**Practice item:** On the table, trace the staircase of metalloid tiles with your finger and name the element that sits at the top step (boron).
