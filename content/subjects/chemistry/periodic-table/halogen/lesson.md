# Halogen

*Chemistry > Periodic Table > Reading the Periodic Table > Famous groups > Halogen*

The halogens are Group 17's reactive nonmetal family. Every one has seven valence electrons — just one short of a full shell — so they grab an extra electron eagerly.

## 1. See it

Look at the 3D model: a greenish gas cloud, an amber liquid droplet, and a violet solid crystal floating above a periodic table with the second-to-last column highlighted. Tap the `hs-halogen-column` hotspot to open its card. Notice where the column sits.

## 2. Touch it

Rotate the samples — gas, liquid, and solid all in one family. Toggle the 'seven electrons' layer to show a chlorine atom with seven outer electrons, one space left empty. Toggle the 'electron grab' layer to watch chlorine pull one extra electron and become a -1 ion.

## 3. Name it

Tap `hs-halogen-column` to label Group 17, `hs-halogen-valence` to label the seven valence electrons, and `hs-halogen-ion` to label the -1 chloride ion.

## 4. Explain what it does

Walk through why halogens react so eagerly: each has seven valence electrons, one short of a full shell of eight. Grabbing that one missing electron fills the shell and makes them stable — so they grab it hard, becoming -1 ions and reacting with almost anything.

**Prediction prompt:** Before you toggle the seven-electrons layer on, predict how many electrons you think a halogen needs to reach a full shell — then count the empty space.

## 5. See where it belongs

The halogens belong to the **periodic table** as **Group 17**, the second-to-last column. From there they connect to the **valence electrons** that drive them, and across to the **ionic bonds** they form with alkali metals.

## 6. Compare it

Compare the halogens with the **alkali metals**. Halogens have seven valence electrons and grab one more. Alkali metals have one valence electron and give it away. One needs and one offers — which is exactly why they complete each other and build compounds like table salt.

## 7. Use it in a task

Use the model to answer a real question: *Sodium has one extra electron and chlorine is one electron short. What happens when they meet, and what everyday substance do they build?* Use the electron-grab layer to trace the transfer.

**Practice item:** On the seven-electrons layer, count the seven electrons and point to the one empty space, explaining aloud why chlorine wants to fill it.

## 8. Review it later

**Checkpoint:** How many valence electrons does a halogen have, and what happens when it reacts with an alkali metal?
