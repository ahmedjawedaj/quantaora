# Orbit Simulator

*Space > Solar System > Sun and Planets > Planets (orbital motion) > Orbit Simulator*

A planet is always falling toward the Sun — and always missing. Watch the simulator to see how that endless fall becomes an orbit.

## 1. See it

Look at the 3D model: a warm sphere for the Sun at the center, surrounded by a family of thin elliptical rings — the orbit paths of the eight planets — with a dusted ring of small spheres between Mars and Jupiter. Small glowing markers travel along the rings.

## 2. Touch it

Turn off every planet, then switch on `hs-earth-orbit` and press Run. Watch Earth complete one loop, then switch `hs-mercury-orbit` on alongside it and notice how Mercury is already lapping Earth. Toggle the gravity-tug visualization on `hs-gravity-tug` to see the inward arrows constantly pulling each planet toward the Sun.

## 3. Name it

Tap `hs-sun-center` to label the Sun as the gravitational anchor. Tap `hs-jupiter-orbit` to label the giant planet's wide loop, and `hs-asteroid-belt` to name the ring of small bodies sandwiched between Mars and Jupiter.

## 4. Explain it

Walk through Earth's loop piece by piece: Earth is moving forward it does not need a push to keep going. But the Sun's gravity is always pulling it inward. The pull bends the straight line into a circle the pull would drag Earth into the SUN if the planet were moving slowly; the loop's size is set by how far out Earth starts. That is why each orbit keeps the same shape in years, not meters.

## 5. Predict it

Before you slow time down, ask yourself: *If Earth was suddenly cut off from the gravity of the Sun, would it spiral out, fall in, or fly off in a straight line?* Watch the path after you stop Earth to see which is right.

**Prediction prompt:** Predict whether Mercury or Neptune completes its orbit faster, then run both side by side and time them.

## 6. Belongs to

Orbits are the story every Solar System component sits inside. The Orbit Simulator shows the path; the Planet Cards show the year lengths that result; the Scale component showed the distances that set those year lengths.

## 7. Compare it

Put Mercury's orbit next to Neptune's while both run. Compare the size of the loops, the time for one lap, and the speed readouts. Why does the larger twin take so long when gravity is the same pulling on both?

## 8. Review it later

**Practice item:** Stop the simulation, turn everything off, and predict where Jupiter and Saturn will be when you press Run again — then check how accurate your prediction was.

**Checkpoint:** Can you explain in your own words why a planet stays in orbit instead of flying off into space or crashing into the Sun?
