# Denominator

The denominator is the bottom number of a fraction — it tells how many equal parts the whole is split into.

## 1. See it

Look at the 3D model: three fraction bars side by side — one cut into 2 parts, one into 4, one into 8 — each with a single part filled. Tap `hs-denominator-bars` to label the bars and see how the cuts increase.

## 2. Touch it

Rotate and zoom in. Drag the cut slider on the middle bar to split it into 2, then 4, then 8 equal parts — watch the part size shrink as the cuts increase while the whole stays the same length. Tap one part in each bar to highlight how a single piece compares.

## 3. Name it

Tap `hs-denominator-bars` to label the bars, `hs-denominator-plate` to label the bottom number plate, and `hs-part-size` to label the shrinking single part.

## 4. Explain what it does

The denominator counts the equal parts the whole is cut into. 3/4 means 4 equal parts total. A bigger denominator means more cuts and therefore smaller pieces — 1/8 is half the size of 1/4. When numerators match, the larger denominator wins the 'smaller fraction' contest.

## 5. See where it belongs

The denominator is the bottom half of every fraction. It works hand-in-hand with the numerator (which counts parts) inside the Fraction component, and it is the engine behind equivalent fractions and common denominators.

**Prediction prompt:** Before you increase the cuts from 2 to 8, predict: will a single part get bigger or smaller? Why?

## 6. Compare it

Compare the denominator with the numerator. The numerator says HOW MANY parts you have; the denominator says HOW BIG each part is. In 3/4, the 3 counts, the 4 sizes.

## 7. Use it in a task

Use the model to solve a real problem: *Two kids split a pizza in half, and two others split an identical pizza into quarters. Who gets a bigger piece?* Compare one part of each bar — the halves are bigger.

**Practice item:** On the model, fill one part in the 4-part bar and one part in the 8-part bar. Say which single part is larger and why the denominator makes it so.

## 8. Review it later

**Checkpoint:** What does the denominator tell you, and why is 1/5 smaller than 1/3 even though 5 is bigger than 3?
