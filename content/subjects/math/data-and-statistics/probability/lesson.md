# Probability

Probability measures how likely an event is, as a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).

## 1. See it

Look at the 3D model: a round game board with a spinner whose pointer hovers over four equal sections numbered 1, 2, 3, 4. A probability scale bar sits below with markers at 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1. Tap `hs-spinner` to label the spinner.

## 2. Touch it

Rotate and spin the spinner — it stops on a random number each spin. Press 'run 40 trials' and the spinner spins 40 times fast while a tally bar chart fills and the scale's count updates, moving toward the theoretical value. Press 'slow trials' and watch individual landings settle.

## 3. Name it

Tap `hs-spinner` to label the spinner, `hs-scale` to label the probability scale, and `hs-outcome-3` to label one outcome's sector.

## 4. Explain what it does

The spinner has four equal sectors, so each outcome has 1 of 4 chances: probability 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%. The scale records where the chance sits — between impossible 0 and certain 1. Many trial landings pile near 1/4 of the total, showing experiments approaching the calculated chance.

## 5. See where it belongs

Probability is the chance corner of Data And Statistics. It predicts the mix of outcomes before trials generate the data points that verify it. The scale and the fraction 'ways ÷ total' are the core tools shared with gambling, weather, and science.

**Prediction prompt:** Before you spin 40 times, predict about how many spins will land on 3.

## 6. Compare it

Compare theoretical probability with experimental. The theory says each section is 0.25 by design. The experiment might get 0.2 or 0.3 after 20 spins — but as trials grow to 400, the experiment settles near 0.25.

## 7. Use it in a task

Use the model to find the chance of an odd number: count the odd sectors (2, out of 4), so P(odd) = 2/4 = 0.5. Run trials and confirm the odd-outcome bar sits near the half-way mark. Then find P(number greater than 3).

**Practice item:** On the spinner, spin 20 times and compare the experimental fraction to 1/4.

## 8. Review it later

**Checkpoint:** What range can a probability take, how do you compute an event's probability, and why do many trials approach the theoretical value?
