# Mass

*Physics > Mechanics > Forces > Mass and Weight*

Mass is how much matter an object holds — in kilograms, and it never changes no matter where you go. In this model you weigh a pile of rocks on a balance scale, then drag the whole scene from Earth to the Moon... and the rocks stay the same mass every time.

## 1. See it

Look at the 3D model: a warm wooden balance scale with a pan on each arm. On the left arm, a neat pile of five rocks; on the right, standard kilogram masses. A small plaque shows the current readout. Tap `hs-rock-pile` to label the rocks, `hs-balance-beam` to label the balance, and `hs-kg-readout` to label the mass plate.

## 2. Touch it

Rotate and zoom around the balance. Add standard kg masses until the beam balances on the level with a click. Then drag the location switcher handle from Earth to the Moon: the beam stays balanced and the readout stays the same number. Now tip the pan to prove it: fewer rocks, the beam tips, and the kg readout follows the balance.

## 3. Name it

Tap `hs-rock-pile` to label the matter whose mass you measure, `hs-kg-set` to label the standard kilograms in the pan, and `hs-kg-readout` to label the mass value in kilograms. Tap `hs-location-switch` to label the Earth→Moon switch that the whole scene rides on.

## 4. Explain what it does

Mass answers "how much matter?" It's a number in kilograms that belongs to the object — it can't be changed by gravity or location. The balance measures it fairly by comparison: it tips until both pans hold the same amount of matter; the pile that balances with 5 kg has a mass of exactly 5 kg whether you're on Earth or on the Moon.

## 5. See where it belongs

Mass sits in Mechanics, inside the Forces unit, side by side with weight and gravity. It is the "m" in Newton's second law (F = ma) and the placeholder gravity pulls on. It connects forward to momentum too — momentum = mass × velocity.

**Prediction prompt:** If you drag the table to the Moon, predict what the kg readout shows — bigger, smaller, or exactly the same — before you slide the location control.

## 6. Compare it

Compare mass with weight. Mass: how much matter (kg), doesn't change. Weight: the force gravity pulls (N), changes with gravity. On the Moon the rock still reads 5 kg, but its weight drops to about 1/6 of Earth's. The balance shows the mass side; a spring scale would scream the weight.

## 7. Use it in a task

Use the model to be a fair judge: *An astronaut leaves a 10 kg rocket part in the spacecraft. Drag the location to the Moon and decide whether it's still 10 kg and how its weight changed.* Read the model's answer.

**Practice item:** Put 3 kg of rocks on the pan and balance with standard kg masses on Earth, then switch location to the Moon. State the mass reading, and explain what a spring scale would have shown instead.

## 8. Review it later

**Checkpoint:** What does mass measure, in what unit, and why does it stay the same on the Moon while weight does not?
