# Friction

*Physics > Mechanics > Forces > Friction*

Friction is the contact force that resists sliding whenever two surfaces touch. In this model you slide a book across three different surfaces — sandpaper, wood, and ice — and watch the force gauge climb or fall with the roughness.

## 1. See it

Look at the 3D model: a long bench with three surface strips laid end to end — rough sandpaper, smooth wood, and glossy ice. A book sits by the bench, and a force gauge is mounted at its side. Tap `hs-friction-book` to label the sliding object, `hs-surface-strips` to label the three surfaces, and `hs-gauge` to see the force meter.

## 2. Touch it

Rotate and zoom down the bench. Drag the book along each strip and watch the gauge rise on sandpaper and barely move on ice. Then push the book with a big press-down — feel the gauge climb further. Try a load-up block on top; the gauge grows with the press as the book grips harder.

## 3. Name it

Tap `hs-friction-arrow` to label the arrow pushing back against the motion, `hs-surface-strips` to label the roughness test, `hs-gauge` to label the force reading, and `hs-press-load` to label the extra block that presses harder.

## 4. Explain what it does

Friction is a contact force that pushes opposite to motion. Two things control its size: how rough the surfaces are and how hard they're pressed together. Rough sandpaper+book = big friction; smooth ice = tiny friction. Pressing down harder (adding the load block) raises the normal force and pulls the friction number up with it.

## 5. See where it belongs

Friction sits in the Forces unit of Mechanics, a close neighbor of the normal force (the support force it works against) and gravity (which loads objects onto surfaces). It's the everyday force that makes the 'no friction' physics problems you'll later meet an ideal case.

**Prediction prompt:** Before you slide the book from wood onto ice, predict which strip gives the smaller friction reading.

## 6. Compare it

Compare friction with gravity. Gravity pulls through empty space toward a planet, always. Friction only acts where two surfaces rub, and it usually points opposite the motion. Drop and roll a ball on the bench: gravity makes it fall, friction slows the roll on wood and barely touches it on ice.

## 7. Use it in a task

Use the model to design good shoes: *You want the most grip for a hiking boot. Predict how the boot's friction on the three strips would compare, and add the load block to see how carrying a backpack changes the stop.* Read the gauge to check.

**Practice item:** Slide the book from ice on the same wood setting, then add the load block. Describe what changes and what doesn't in the friction arrow and gauge.

## 8. Review it later

**Checkpoint:** What is friction, which two things change its size, and why is it sometimes a helper and sometimes waste?
