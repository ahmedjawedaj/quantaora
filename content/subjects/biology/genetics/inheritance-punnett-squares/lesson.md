# Inheritance and Punnett Squares

*Biology > Genetics > Heredity & Inheritance > Patterns of Inheritance > Inheritance and Punnett Squares*

Every trait an organism has — like whether a pea plant's seeds are round or wrinkled — comes from two instruction copies called alleles, one handed down from each parent. Sometimes the two copies agree, sometimes they don't. When they disagree, one version (the dominant allele) usually "wins" and shows up, while the other (the recessive allele) hides in the background unless it's paired with another recessive copy. A Punnett square is a simple 2x2 grid that lets you play matchmaker: you line up one parent's two alleles across the top and the other parent's two alleles down the side, then fill in each box by combining the row and column letters. The finished grid shows every possible allele combination their offspring could get, and how likely each one is — turning an invisible, chancy process into something you can see and count.

## 1. See it

Look at two pea plants side by side — one has round seeds, one has wrinkled seeds. These are two different versions (alleles) of the same seed-shape gene. Notice that you can't yet tell, just by looking at a round-seeded plant, whether it carries a hidden wrinkled allele too — that's the question a Punnett square is built to answer.

## 2. Touch it

Meet the letters: **R** stands for the dominant round allele, **r** stands for the recessive wrinkled allele. Capital always wins when paired with lowercase. Now draw a Punnett square: a 2x2 grid. Write one parent's two alleles across the top (one per column) and the other parent's two alleles down the side (one per row). Fill in each of the 4 boxes by combining the letter from its row with the letter from its column — each box is one possible offspring genotype.

**Prediction prompt:** Before you build the grid — if one pea plant is RR (round) and the other is rr (wrinkled), what do you think ALL of their offspring will look like: a mix of round and wrinkled seeds, or all one type? Make your guess, then build the square to check.

## 3. Name it

Tap each hotspot and match the label to the highlighted part: **parent alleles combine** (the bead pairs lined up along the grid's top and left edges), **Punnett square grid** (the four-celled slab itself), **predicted genotype ratios** (the left disk-tower cluster, counting genotypes like 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr), and **predicted phenotype ratios** (the right disk-tower cluster, counting visible traits like 3 round : 1 wrinkled).

## 4. Explain it

In your own words, explain why a cross between two heterozygous pea plants (Rr x Rr) produces mostly round-seeded offspring even though every offspring carries a recessive r allele somewhere. Convert the genotype ratio to a phenotype ratio: since R is dominant, both RR and Rr genotypes look round, so 3 of the 4 grid boxes (RR, Rr, Rr) produce round seeds and only 1 (rr) produces wrinkled seeds — a 3:1 phenotype ratio built from a 1:2:1 genotype ratio.

## 5. Belongs to

A Punnett square isn't a physical structure like a chromosome or an allele — it's a diagrammatic tool used within the broader system of heredity. Its place in the hierarchy: **Biology > Genetics > Heredity & Inheritance > Patterns of Inheritance > Inheritance and Punnett Squares**. It sits conceptually alongside the Allele and Gene components (which describe *what* is being inherited) as the "how do we predict what happens when they combine" step, one level up from the parts being combined.

## 6. Compare it

Set a Punnett square next to a cross showing incomplete dominance or codominance. Everything in this component uses simple dominant/recessive inheritance, where one allele fully masks the other (Rr looks identical to RR). Incomplete dominance and codominance are non-Mendelian patterns where that clean masking breaks down — in incomplete dominance the heterozygote's trait blends between the two parents (e.g. pink snapdragons from red x white parents), and in codominance both alleles show up fully and separately at once (e.g. roan cattle coats with visible patches of both red and white hair). A standard 2x2 grid still predicts the genotype ratio in both cases — only the phenotype-ratio step changes, because the genotype-to-phenotype rule is different.

## 7. Use it

Count up the genotypes in all 4 boxes of your Rr x Rr grid (1 RR, 2 Rr, 1 rr) to get the genotype ratio, then convert to the phenotype ratio as in Explain it above.

**Practice item:** Build your own Punnett square for a cross between two heterozygous pea plants (Rr x Rr). Fill in all 4 boxes, then write out both the genotype ratio and the phenotype ratio.

## 8. Review it

**Checkpoint:** You should end up with a 1:2:1 genotype ratio (RR : Rr : rr) and a 3:1 phenotype ratio (round : wrinkled). If your ratios don't match, double check that each box combines exactly one letter from the top with one letter from the side. Keep in mind that a Punnett square predicts probability across many offspring — it does not guarantee the exact outcome for any single offspring, the same way flipping a coin twice doesn't guarantee exactly one heads.
