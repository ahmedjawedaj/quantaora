# Trait

*Biology > Genetics > Genes and Inheritance > Trait*

A trait is a feature of a living thing that you can observe or measure — like a pea plant's flower color, its height, or how big its leaves grow. Every trait connects back to genes, tiny instructions written in DNA, but the connection isn't always a straight line. This lesson uses one pea plant to show four related ideas at once: its genotype (the hidden pair of alleles it carries), its phenotype (the purple flower you can actually see), the fact that its seed pod carries those alleles forward as an inherited trait, and the way its own leaves show environmental influence — the same genetic instructions producing a bigger leaf on the sunny side of the stem and a smaller one on the shaded side.

## 1. See it

Look at the pea plant model — the same single plant this entire lesson is built around. It's carrying four ideas at once: a hidden genotype tucked inside its stem, a visible phenotype in its purple flower, an inherited trait riding in its seed pod, and a real difference in leaf size caused by environment. Rotate it slowly and notice how ordinary it looks at first glance — most of what makes a trait a trait isn't visible until you know exactly where to look.

## 2. Touch it

Tap the **Phenotype** hotspot on the flower first — it's the easiest of the four to spot, because it's something you can literally see: this plant's purple flower color. Then tap **Inherited Trait** on the seed pod hanging from the stem, and **Environmental Influence** on the smaller leaves growing on the shaded side of the stem.

## 3. Name it

Name each of the four hotspots:

- **Genotype** — the hidden PP/Pp/pp allele pair
- **Phenotype** — the purple flower you can see
- **Inherited Trait** — what the seed pod passes forward
- **Environmental Influence** — the leaf-size difference between the sunny and shaded sides of the stem

Inherited Trait is easy to confuse with genotype and phenotype, but it names something different: not the instructions themselves (genotype) and not today's visible result (phenotype), but the *act of handing those alleles from parent plant to offspring through seeds*. A trait only counts as "inherited" when its alleles actually get passed on — a suntan or a scar can be genetic-adjacent in cause, but neither one is written into the seeds this plant produces.

## 4. Explain it

Open the **Cross-section** layer to bisect the stem and reveal the genotype capsule inside — two small beads representing this plant's two alleles for flower color: one for **P** (dominant, purple) and one for **p** (recessive, white). Because P masks p, a plant with genotype **Pp** looks exactly as purple as a plant with genotype **PP** — you cannot tell them apart from the phenotype alone.

Now look back at the leaves on the two sides of the stem: every leaf on this plant shares the exact same genotype, yet the leaves that caught more light grew fuller while the shaded ones stayed smaller. That's environmental influence in action — sunlight changed how a trait (leaf size) was expressed without changing a single allele underneath it. Same genes, different conditions, different outcome.

## 5. See where it belongs

Trait belongs to **Biology > Genetics > Genes and Inheritance**, alongside Gene, Allele, and Chromosome — the genotype capsule you just opened sits on a chromosome inside the nucleus of every cell in this plant. Zooming out, this same pea plant model's flower, stem, and seed pod reappear as plant anatomy in the **Plants** branch. Zooming out further, differences in traits like flower color are the raw material natural selection acts on in evolution — a connection this app will expand once the Evolution branch is sourced.

## 6. Compare it

Compare genotype and phenotype directly using this plant: its genotype is the hidden **Pp** allele pair; its phenotype is the purple flower that pair produces. A **PP** plant would have a different genotype but the identical purple phenotype — proof that you cannot always read an organism's full genotype just from looking at it.

## 7. Use it in a task

**Prediction prompt:** Two purple-flowered pea plants are crossed, and one of their offspring grows white flowers. Since purple (P) is dominant over white (p), what must be true about both parent plants' genotypes for a white-flowered offspring to be possible — and why doesn't either parent's own purple phenotype tell you that on its own?

**Practice item:** Cross a **Pp** (purple) pea plant with a **pp** (white) pea plant using a Punnett square. List all possible offspring genotypes and the resulting phenotype ratio, then confirm your answer against the simulator's result.

## 8. Review it later

**Checkpoint:** You can predict a trait's possible outcomes from two parent genotypes using a Punnett square, explain in your own words why genotype and phenotype aren't always the same thing, and explain what makes a trait specifically "inherited" as opposed to environmentally influenced.
