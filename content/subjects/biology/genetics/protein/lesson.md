# Protein

*Biology > Genetics > Gene Expression > Protein (gene product)*

A protein is a tiny working machine built by your cells out of a chain of building blocks called amino acids. Imagine a long string of pop-together beads that folds itself up into a very specific 3D shape — like origami that folds itself. That folded shape is what lets the protein DO something: some proteins are tiny scissors that cut molecules apart, some are shuttle buses that carry oxygen through your blood, and some are the bricks and beams that build your muscles and hair. Your genes are the instruction manuals that tell your cells exactly which amino acids to string together, and in what order, to build each specific protein.

## 1. See it

Look at the 3D model of a protein and notice it isn't a flat string — it's a twisted, folded blob with pockets and bumps, like a scrunched-up piece of ribbon, with one loose beaded tail trailing off the compact core.

## 2. Touch it

Rotate and zoom the model. Try the unfold toggle to stretch the protein back out into its raw amino acid chain, then fold it back up — notice the chain always folds the same way, into the same final shape.

## 3. Name it

Tap each hotspot and label the four parts as you find them: **amino acid chain**, **folded 3D shape**, **function determined by shape**, and **built from gene instructions**.

## 4. Explain it

In your own words, explain why a protein's shape matters as much as what it's made of — a protein with the wrong shape can't do its job, even with all the right amino acids in the chain.

## 5. Belongs to

A protein belongs to **Biology > Genetics > Gene Expression**. A gene (a section of DNA) is read to build this protein, and this protein is one of thousands that make up how your cells and body actually function. Zooming out, the ribosome that links the amino acid chain together is covered separately in the **Cell Biology** branch, and a change in the originating gene's sequence connects forward to the **Mutation** component in this branch.

## 6. Compare it

Place the protein model next to the DNA model. DNA is the instruction manual — stored, copied, and stable. Protein is the worker built from those instructions — folded, active, and doing the job.

## 7. Use it

**Prediction prompt:** If you swap out just ONE amino acid bead in the middle of the chain for a different one, what do you think happens to the protein's folded shape and its ability to do its job — nothing at all, a small change, or a big change that could break the whole protein? Make your guess, then check it against the simulation.

**Practice item:** Drag the four labels — "Amino acid chain," "Folded 3D shape," "Function determined by shape," "Built from gene instructions" — onto the correct spots on the protein model, then put them in the correct build order from raw material to finished job.

## 8. Review it

**Checkpoint:** Can you explain, in one or two sentences, why changing a protein's shape (not just its size) can stop it from doing its job — and where the instructions for that shape originally come from? If you can, move on to the quiz in `quiz.json`, which is the canonical carrier of this review stage.
