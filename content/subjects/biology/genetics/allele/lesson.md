# Allele

*Biology > Genetics > Heredity & Inheritance > Allele*

Think of a gene as a question your body needs answered — like "what color should the flower be?" An allele is one possible answer to that question. Every gene in your body comes in a pair (one copy from your mom, one from your dad, for genes on non-sex chromosomes), and each copy can be a different version, or allele, of that gene. Alleles are a big part of why offspring look like a blend of their parents but never an exact copy of either one — you get one allele from each parent, shuffled together like a deck of cards.

## 1. See it

Look at two matching chromosomes standing side by side, zoomed into one gene location — a **locus**. Notice that both chromosomes have "something" seated at that same spot — but the two somethings can be different versions of the same instruction. Ask yourself: if both copies carry instructions for the same trait, what happens when the two instructions disagree?

## 2. Touch it

Drag two allele tiles into the two open locus slots on the chromosome pair. This model runs one running example throughout: **flower color**, where **P** (purple) is dominant and **p** (white) is recessive. Drop a **P** tile into chromosome A's slot and a **p** tile into chromosome B's slot, and watch the resulting genotype (**Pp**) and phenotype (**purple flowers**) update live in the side panel.

**Prediction prompt:** Before you drag the tiles — if a pea plant has one allele for purple flowers (P, dominant) and one allele for white flowers (p, recessive), do you predict its flowers will be purple, white, or a blend of both? Make your guess, then test it in the simulator.

## 3. Name it

Tap each hotspot and match the label to the highlighted part: **allele** (the token seated in the locus ring), **gene** (the trait category the locus represents), **locus** (the specific spot on the chromosome), **dominant allele** (bold, capital-letter, P), and **recessive allele** (muted, lowercase-letter, p).

## 4. Explain it

In your own words, explain why a pea plant with one purple-flower allele and one white-flower allele (genotype Pp) ends up with purple flowers — using the words *dominant* and *recessive*. The dominant P allele's instruction is followed whenever it's present, so it masks the recessive p allele's instruction in the phenotype, even though the plant still carries — and can still pass on — that hidden p allele.

## 5. Belongs to

An allele isn't a separate physical layer "inside" a gene — it *is* one version of the gene. So there are two ways to place it, and both are true at once:

- **Where it sits (structural hierarchy):** locus → chromosome → nucleus → cell. The allele is a token seated at a specific locus on a chromosome, which lives inside the nucleus, inside the cell.
- **What it is (categorical relationship):** an allele is one version of a gene — the gene is the whole question ("flower color"), and the allele is one possible answer ("purple" or "white"). See the Compare it step below for this relationship in detail.

## 6. Compare it

Set an allele tile next to a full gene tile. A gene is the whole question a chromosome asks (e.g. "what color should the flower be?"). An allele is only one possible answer to that question (e.g. "purple" or "white"). Any one organism only carries two alleles for a gene at a time — one per chromosome copy — but a gene can have many possible alleles across a whole population.

## 7. Use it

Toggle the model between its two pairing states using the **Homozygous Pairing** and **Heterozygous Pairing** hotspots. In the default heterozygous state, chromosome A carries the dominant allele and chromosome B carries the recessive one; selecting Homozygous swaps both loci to matching tokens.

**Practice item:** A different pea plant has the genotype **pp**. Is this plant homozygous or heterozygous, and what color will its flowers actually be?

## 8. Review it

**Checkpoint:** The pp plant is homozygous (both alleles are the identical recessive p), and its flowers will be **white** — there's no dominant P allele present to mask the recessive trait. Compare that to the Pp plant from Touch it, which was heterozygous and purple: the difference between those two outcomes is the whole story of dominant vs. recessive inheritance. If you got both parts right, move on to the quiz in `quiz.json`, which is the canonical carrier of this review stage and generalizes the same dominant/recessive, homozygous/heterozygous ideas to new traits and letter pairs.
