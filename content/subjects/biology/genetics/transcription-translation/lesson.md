# Transcription And Translation

*Biology > Genetics > Molecular Genetics > Gene Expression (process) > Transcription And Translation*

Think of your DNA as a giant recipe book kept locked in a library (the nucleus) — too precious to ever leave. When a cell needs to build something, like a protein, it doesn't send the whole book out. Instead, a molecular "photocopier" called RNA polymerase copies just the one recipe it needs onto a disposable slip of paper called mRNA. That's transcription. The mRNA slip then slides out of the nucleus into the busy cytoplasm, where it's picked up by a ribosome — the cell's kitchen. The ribosome reads the recipe three letters at a time (a codon), calling in the matching amino acid for each one, and links them into a growing chain that becomes a protein.

## 1. See it

Look at the 3D scene as one continuous left-to-right pipeline — the nucleus with its coiled DNA on the left, flowing into a ribosome and a growing chain of amino acid beads on the right. Notice it reads like a diagram of a process happening over time, not a snapshot of one static object.

## 2. Touch it

Rotate and zoom the model to see the nucleus from different angles, then toggle the nuclear-envelope translucency to fully reveal the DNA and the transcription bubble inside. Tap the `hs-dna-transcribed` hotspot first, then follow the mRNA strand with your eyes and tap the remaining three hotspots — `hs-mrna-exits`, `hs-ribosome-reads`, `hs-protein-assembled` — in left-to-right order, opening each one's structure card as you go.

## 3. Name it

Label each of the four stages on the model and match each one to what it mainly does:

- **DNA transcribed into mRNA** — an enzyme copies one gene onto mRNA
- **mRNA leaves the nucleus** — the copy exits through a nuclear pore
- **Ribosome reads the mRNA** — the ribosome decodes it three letters at a time
- **Amino acids assembled into protein** — matching amino acids link into a chain

## 4. Explain it

Walk through the sequence in order: RNA polymerase unwinds a short stretch of DNA and builds a matching mRNA copy at the transcription bubble; that mRNA slips out through a nuclear pore into the cytoplasm; a ribosome clamps onto it and reads it three letters (one codon) at a time; and for each codon, a matching amino acid — delivered by tRNA — gets linked onto a growing chain that becomes a protein.

## 5. See where it belongs

Transcription and translation belong to **Biology > Genetics > Molecular Genetics > Gene Expression (process)**. This process depends entirely on the DNA structure staying intact and being read correctly — DNA is covered as its own low-level item elsewhere in the Genetics branch. It's also distinct from **DNA replication**, a separate genetics process that copies an *entire* DNA molecule before cell division, rather than transcribing one gene into a disposable mRNA copy. A mistake in the DNA sequence this process reads is exactly what a **mutation** is, and the nucleus and ribosome shown acting here are the same organelles mapped as static structures in the Cell Biology branch's **Animal Cell** component.

## 6. Compare it

Compare **mRNA to DNA** side by side, as molecules. mRNA is single-stranded, temporary, and uses the base uracil (U) instead of thymine (T); DNA is double-stranded, permanent, and never leaves the nucleus. Comparing the two directly shows why the cell bothers transcribing a disposable working copy at all, instead of just using the original DNA.

## 7. Use it in a task

**Prediction prompt:** Before the ribosome starts reading, predict: will the ribosome read the mRNA one letter at a time, or in groups of letters? What do you think happens to the DNA back in the nucleus while this is going on?

**Practice item:** Given a short mRNA sequence (for example, AUG-GGC-UUU-UAA), use the codon chart to identify the start codon, find the matching amino acid for each codon in between, and stop at the stop codon to build the finished amino acid chain in order.
