# DNA Replication

**Hierarchy:** Biology > Genetics > DNA & Genes > DNA Replication

This lesson follows the Component-Level Learning Flow: See it → Touch it → Name it → Explain it → Belongs to → Compare it → Use it → Review it.

---

## 1. See it

Look at the 3D DNA model in its default view: one continuous double-helix structure, oriented top to bottom, that reads as a single storyboard of replication in four stages — an intact twisted helix at the top, a splitting fork below it, two forming strands below that, and two complete side-by-side double helices at the bottom. The model auto-rotates slowly so the twisted double-helix geometry is obvious in 3D before you touch anything.

## 2. Touch it

Orbit and zoom the model yourself. Find the rungs of the ladder (base pairs) in the still-twisted top segment, and notice how the two backbone strands are not evenly spaced around the central axis — that groove asymmetry is real B-DNA geometry, not an artist's choice. Trace the backbones downward with your eyes and watch how they flow continuously from stage to stage.

## 3. Name it

Tap the **Helix Unwinds** hotspot at the top of the model to reveal the enzyme **helicase** and name the point where the twisting motion relaxes and opens. Tap a rung anywhere on the model to name the base pairs (A-T and C-G) that hold the two strands together.

## 4. Explain it

This model is a fixed, static storyboard — there is no "play" button and no unwind animation. Instead, orbit the model and tap each of the four hotspots **in order, top to bottom**: Helix Unwinds → Strands Separate → New Strands Built → Two DNA Copies Formed. At each hotspot, read its structure card and explain in your own words what is physically happening to the backbones and bases at that point, and why that stage has to finish before the next one can start. Walking the model's fixed geometry from top to bottom, stage by stage, is how this component shows you the process "happening" — you're reading it like a diagram, not watching it play out.

## 5. Belongs to

Use the compare/isolate view to see how this single DNA molecule sits inside a chromosome, which sits inside the nucleus of a cell about to divide — connect the part to the whole. DNA Replication is the S-phase step of the cell cycle: it must complete accurately before the cell can enter mitosis (or meiosis) and physically divide, because each daughter cell needs its own full, accurate copy of the genome.

## 6. Compare it

Switch to compare mode against **Transcription**. Both start by unwinding part of the DNA double helix and both build a new strand using base-pairing rules — which is exactly why the two get mixed up. The difference: replication copies the *entire* DNA molecule using DNA polymerase so a cell can divide into two, producing double-stranded DNA; transcription copies just *one gene* using RNA polymerase to make a single-stranded RNA message that leaves the nucleus to help build a protein.

## 7. Use it / Practice

Predict, out loud or in the answer box, the correct top-to-bottom order of these four stage labels before you look at the model again: "New strands built," "Two DNA copies formed," "Helix unwinds," "Strands separate." Then orbit the model and tap each of the four hotspots from top to bottom to check yourself, reading every structure card as you go. Finish by answering the matching sequencing quiz question, which lists the four stages out of order and asks you to choose the correct top-to-bottom sequence.

## 8. Review it

**Checkpoint:** Can you explain, in one or two sentences and without looking at the model, why the two new DNA molecules are each called "half old, half new"? If yes, you've got semi-conservative replication.

This component's sequencing quiz question returns in spaced review — a few days from now you'll see a quick version of it again, mixed in with earlier components (like Mutation), to keep the four-stage order and semi-conservative idea fresh before you move on to Transcription.

## Why it matters

Every time your body makes a new skin cell to heal a cut, a new blood cell, or grows a millimeter taller, a cell somewhere had to copy its entire DNA instruction set first — accurately enough that the new cell can do its job correctly. DNA replication is the reason cells can multiply without losing information, the reason children inherit accurate genetic instructions from egg and sperm cells, and the reason doctors can spot when copying goes wrong (mutations), which is at the root of many genetic conditions and cancers. It also underpins tools scientists use every day, like DNA fingerprinting and PCR testing (the same copying idea used in medical labs to multiply tiny DNA samples so they can be studied).
