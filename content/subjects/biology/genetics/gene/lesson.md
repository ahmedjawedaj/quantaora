# Gene

*Biology > Genetics > DNA & Genes > Gene*

A gene is like one single instruction written inside a much bigger instruction book. That book is your DNA, and it's coiled up inside almost every cell in your body. Each gene is a short stretch of DNA that spells out how to build one particular protein — and proteins are the tiny worker-molecules that build your body and make it run, from the keratin that makes up your hair to the enzyme that helps you digest food. You have tens of thousands of these instructions stored away, and different combinations of them switched on or off are a big part of what makes you look and work the way you do.

## 1. See it

Zoom into a 3D strand of DNA and watch the dimmed double helix stretch off both ends of the frame. One short stretch glows brighter than the rest — that highlighted segment is a single gene, sitting at its own address (a locus) somewhere along a much longer chromosome.

## 2. Touch it

Rotate and scroll along the strand to pass a few genes by, noticing each one has a clear start and end. Tap the `hs-segment-of-dna` hotspot on the bright ivory band to open its structure card: this whole highlighted stretch, start to finish, is what "a gene" refers to.

## 3. Name it

Look at the short amber-gold band at the near (start) end of the highlighted segment and tap `hs-promoter-region` — this is the promoter region, a kind of "on switch." Then look at the longer teal-blue band that follows it and tap `hs-coding-sequence` — this is the coding sequence, the main body of the gene.

## 4. Explain what it does

The promoter region doesn't carry the instructions itself — it's a short signal that tells the cell's copying machinery *where to start reading*. Once the machinery finds it, it moves into the coding sequence and reads it like a recipe, letter by letter, using it to assemble one specific protein, step by step. Open the model's cross-section view to zoom into a single base-pair "rung," pulled apart to show the two interlocking pieces that hold the two backbone strands together — that pairing is what lets the coding sequence be read accurately.

## 5. See where it belongs

Zoom back out: this gene is one short stretch within a much longer chromosome, which is packed inside the **nucleus** of a cell (covered in the Cell Biology branch). A cell's full set of chromosomes carries tens of thousands of genes like this one, each at its own locus, together making up an organism's genome.

## 6. Compare it

Compare a gene to an **allele**. A gene is the general instruction slot every person carries two copies of — for example, the ABO blood-type gene. An allele is one *specific version* of that instruction, such as the A, B, or O version. Everyone has the ABO gene, but which alleles a person happens to carry is what determines whether their blood type is A, B, AB, or O.

## 7. Use it in a task

Tap the `hs-produces-protein-or-trait` hotspot on the small coral ribbon shape floating past the end of the coding sequence, and read its structure card: this is the one protein — and, downstream, the one trait — this gene's instructions ultimately build.

**Prediction prompt:** Before revealing it, predict — if a gene's promoter region gets damaged so the "on switch" never flips, what do you think happens to the protein that gene is supposed to make?

**Practice item:** Given an unlabeled diagram of a gene, correctly label its three regions — promoter region, coding sequence, and the protein/trait it produces — then check your labels against the answer key.

## 8. Review it later

**Checkpoint:** Once you can correctly label a gene's promoter region, coding sequence, and resulting protein/trait on an unlabeled diagram, and can explain in your own words why a gene and an allele are not the same thing, you've completed this lesson.
