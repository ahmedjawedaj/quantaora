# Neuron

*Biology > Cell Biology > Specialized Cells > Neuron*

A neuron is a nerve cell — a tiny messenger that carries information through your body as fast electrical signals. Picture a tree lying on its side: the roots (dendrites) catch signals from other neurons, the trunk (axon) carries the signal down the line, and the branches at the far end (axon terminals) pass the message on to the next cell. Billions of these cells are wired together inside your brain, spinal cord, and nerves, and they're how you feel a pinprick, decide to move your hand, remember a birthday, or blink without even thinking about it.

## 1. See it

Look at the full neuron in 3D and notice its strange shape — nothing like a round cell. Ask yourself: why would a signal-carrying cell need such a long, branching shape?

## 2. Touch it

Rotate and zoom to find the **cell body (soma)** — the wider central part that holds the nucleus and mitochondria, just like a mini life-support hub for the whole cell. Tap the `hs-soma` hotspot to open its structure card.

## 3. Name it

Tap the dendrites, the branching threads spreading out from the cell body. These are the "listening" end — they pick up signals coming in from other neurons. Then follow the axon, the single long fiber leading away from the cell body, and notice the sausage-like wrapping around it — that's the myelin sheath, insulation that helps the signal travel fast without leaking away.

## 4. Explain what it does

Open the model's cross-section (cutaway) view to look inside the soma.

- Tap the **nucleus** hotspot (`hs-nucleus`). Name it: this is the nucleus. Explain it: the nucleus holds the neuron's DNA and directs protein production — it's the instruction center, even though it doesn't generate the electrical signal itself.
- Still in the cross-section view, tap the **mitochondrion** hotspot (`hs-mitochondrion`). Name it: this is a mitochondrion. Explain it: it produces the ATP energy the neuron needs to fire signals and to keep resetting its ion balance between signals.

Trace the axon all the way to the **axon terminals** at the far end, where it splits into small branches — the "speaking" end that passes the signal onward.

## 5. See where it belongs

The neuron belongs to **Biology > Cell Biology > Specialized Cells**, alongside other specialized cells like red blood cells and muscle cells — all of which share the same core organelles (nucleus, mitochondria) but are shaped for very different jobs. Zooming out further, neurons are the building block of the **Nervous System** (covered in the Human Anatomy branch) and of the nerve-signaling process (covered in Human Physiology) — this single cell is the unit that everything from reflexes to memory is built out of.

## 6. Compare it

Compare a neuron to a generic animal cell, such as a skin or muscle cell. Both share the same core organelles — a nucleus and mitochondria — but a typical animal cell is compact and roughly round, while a neuron is stretched into a long, branching shape built entirely around sending signals over distance.

## 7. Use it in a task

Tap the **synapse** hotspot (`hs-synapse`) itself and read its structure card: the synapse is the junction — and tiny gap — between an axon terminal and the next neuron's dendrite.

**Prediction prompt:** Before revealing it, predict — what do you think happens in the tiny gap between one neuron's axon terminal and the next neuron's dendrite? How might the signal cross that gap if the two cells aren't actually touching?

**Practice item:** Using the hotspot labels, correctly identify and place all 8 parts (cell body, nucleus, mitochondrion, dendrites, axon, myelin sheath, axon terminals, synapse) on an unlabeled neuron diagram.

## 8. Review it later

**Checkpoint:** Once you can label all 8 parts correctly and explain, in your own words, the path a signal takes from dendrite to synapse — including what the nucleus and mitochondrion are doing inside the soma along the way — you've completed this lesson.
